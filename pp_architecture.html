Overview

Introduction & Personal Interest

I spend approximately seven hours a day inside school buildings. Yet despite the significant portion of my adolescence spent in classrooms, I had never stopped to question why those spaces looked and felt the way they did. The trigger came during a geography unit on human geography, when I read about how urban planners design public spaces to encourage or discourage certain behaviours. It struck me that the same logic must apply to educational environments — and if it did, what did the research say about the kind of environments that actually support learning?

I began observing my own school with new eyes. The library was quiet but unwelcoming — bright fluorescent lighting, rows of identical chairs facing the same direction, no natural light from the side of the room facing outdoors. The design appeared to have been chosen for efficiency rather than for the psychological needs of the students using it. Was this accidental, or had the designers simply not been aware of the growing body of research on environmental psychology in educational settings?

This curiosity became my learning goal: to develop an informed understanding of how physical learning environments affect student wellbeing, focus, and social interaction — drawing on environmental psychology, space design theory, and educational architecture — in order to apply that understanding to a design proposal and scale model for an improved learning space.

Key distinction: The learning goal is understanding environmental psychology and its application to educational space design. The product goal is producing a scale architectural model. These are related but not the same — the research could exist without the model, and the model only has value because of the research it applies.

Criterion A — Planning

Planning the Personal Project

Criterion A: 8 marks total (Ai + Aii + Aiii)

Ai · Learning Goal & Global Context

Learning Goal

My learning goal is to understand how the physical design of learning spaces — including factors such as natural light, acoustic quality, spatial flexibility, colour, and furniture configuration — affects student cognitive performance, emotional wellbeing, and social behaviour, and to explore how principles from environmental psychology and educational architecture can be practically applied in a real school redesign context.

This is distinct from my product. The knowledge I am developing — about Kaplan's Attention Restoration Theory, about the evidence on natural light and academic performance, about the principles of biophilic design — would be intellectually valuable whether or not I produced a physical model. The model is the vehicle for applying and demonstrating this understanding.

Global Context: Identities & Relationships

I selected Identities and Relationships, with a focus on the interplay between the physical environment and the development of personal and communal identity during adolescence. Schools are among the primary environments in which young people form their sense of self, their relationships with peers, and their relationship with learning itself. Poorly designed spaces can communicate unintentional messages about how students are valued. A student sitting under harsh fluorescent lighting in a space with no natural view, personal ownership, or acoustic comfort may internalise a message — however unconsciously — that their comfort is not a priority. My project asks: what would a space communicate if it were designed with students' psychological and social needs at the centre?

Aii · Intended Product & Success Criteria

Intended Product

My intended product is a 1:25 scale architectural model of a redesigned secondary school learning space (approximately the size of a standard school library), accompanied by a design report that explains and justifies each design decision with reference to the environmental psychology research. The model will be constructed from foam board, balsa wood, and recycled materials, mounted on a base measuring approximately 60 × 40 cm. The design report (5–8 pages, separate from the PP report) will include annotated floor plans, a materials list, lighting calculations, and a rationale for every major design element.

Aiii · Action Plan

Weeks 1–2
Research — Environmental Psychology & School Design

Read primary literature on Attention Restoration Theory, biophilic design, acoustic impacts on learning, and natural light studies. Apply CRAAP test to all sources. Build an annotated bibliography.

Week 3
Site Observation & Current Space Analysis

Measure and photograph the existing space. Document current conditions against environmental psychology criteria. Identify specific design failures the proposal will address.

Weeks 4–5
Design Development — Floor Plans & Annotations

Produce hand-drawn and digital floor plans at 1:25 scale. Annotate every design decision with a source. Seek teacher feedback on feasibility and spatial logic.

Weeks 6–8
Model Construction

Build the scale model in stages: base and walls first, then furniture and fittings, then detail elements (lighting simulation, plant features). Photograph each stage for appendix evidence.

Week 9
Student Survey & Feedback

Display model to ≥10 school students. Run the comparison survey (current vs. proposed space, 5-point scale). Collect and analyse results (Success Criterion 4).

Weeks 10–12
Report Writing, Review & Exhibition Preparation

Write PP report with full Criterion A, B, C coverage. Final supervisor meeting. Prepare exhibition display with model, floor plans, and survey data visualisation.

Criterion B — Applying Skills

Applying ATL Skills

Criterion B: 8 marks total (Bi + Bii)

Bi · ATL Skills Applied to the Learning Goal

Research Skills — Evaluating Contradictory Evidence

A significant challenge in developing my learning was that the research literature on school environments is not uniform. Heschong Mahone Group (1999) found that students in classrooms with more natural daylight progressed 20% faster in maths and 26% faster in reading. However, subsequent critiques (Seppänen et al., 2006) noted methodological weaknesses in the daylighting correlation: the classrooms with more windows might also have had better teachers or newer buildings overall. I had to learn to evaluate primary studies not just for their conclusions but for the rigour of their methodology — distinguishing correlation from causation, and noting where variables were not adequately controlled. This skill of methodological evaluation was entirely new to me and changed how I now read any research report, academic or otherwise.

Thinking Skills — Applying Theory to Practice

Kaplan's Attention Restoration Theory (ART) proposes that directed attention — the cognitive resource required for focused study — is restored by exposure to "restorative environments" characterised by fascination, extent, being-away, and compatibility. Reading the theoretical model was one challenge; translating it into specific design decisions was another. I had to ask: what does "fascination" look like in a school library? I concluded it could be represented by visual complexity — layered surfaces, varied materials, living plants — rather than the visual monotony of identical furniture rows. This translation from abstract theory to specific, physical design choices was the intellectual core of my project and the primary evidence of my thinking skills development.

Bii · ATL Skills Applied to Creating the Product

Self-Management — Iterative Prototyping & Revision

My first attempt at constructing the acoustic panel walls — using foam board layered with fabric — collapsed when the adhesive failed overnight. Rather than rebuilding the same design, I researched the structural properties of different adhesive types and discovered that PVA glue creates a more flexible bond suitable for layered materials than the hot glue I had been using. I rebuilt the panels using a PVA-balsa wood composite, which held securely. This experience of planned failure and systematic revision was not something I had previously experienced in school project work, where a failed first attempt typically means starting over rather than iterating.

Social Skills — Soliciting and Acting on Critical Feedback

When I shared my initial floor plan with a design-technology teacher, they pointed out that my "collaborative cluster zone" would create significant noise interference for the adjacent "quiet focus zone" — a basic acoustic planning error I had overlooked. My instinct was defensive; I had spent three days on that floor plan. However, I recognised that the teacher's feedback reflected a gap in my knowledge (acoustic zoning principles in space planning) rather than a flaw in my effort. I revised the plan to separate the two zones with the storage and circulation corridor, creating an acoustic buffer. This experience of receiving criticism without defensiveness and acting on it systematically is something I now recognise as a Social skill in the ATL framework: collaborative communication and responsiveness to feedback.

Source Evaluation — CRAAP Test Applied

1
Barrett, P., et al. (2015). The HEAD Project: Holistic Evidence and Design. University of Salford.
Government-funded research report · University of Salford · Peter Barrett: leading researcher in built environment and education

The HEAD project measured over 700 primary school pupils in 34 UK classrooms across 3 years. It identified that 3 environmental parameters — naturalness (light, air, temperature), individualisation (ownership, flexibility), and appropriate stimulation — together accounted for 16% of the variation in pupils' academic progress across a year. Highly credible due to longitudinal design, large sample, and independent academic authorship. Used to justify Success Criteria 1 and 2.

Criterion C — Reflecting

Reflecting on Learning & Product

Criterion C: 8 marks total (Ci + Cii)

Ci · Impact on Learning & Personal Development

Impact 1: From Consumer to Designer of Environments

Before this project, I experienced spaces as a passive user — I moved through them, felt them to be good or bad, but never understood why I felt that way. The moment of transition came when I read Barrett et al.'s finding that "individualisation" — students' sense of ownership over and connection to their physical environment — was one of the three most significant environmental factors in academic progress. It reframed every school space I had ever been in. The bare institutional wall, the furniture bolted to the floor, the absence of any student-made work in the corridor — these were not neutral aesthetic choices. They were, however unintentionally, communicating something about the relationship between the institution and the student.

I became not just a researcher but a kind of environmental critic. This project has permanently changed how I read and interpret built spaces. I am now the person in a café who notices where the natural light enters, or the person in a library who thinks about the acoustic relationship between zones. That perceptual shift feels like the most lasting outcome of the project — not a piece of knowledge I acquired, but a lens through which I now see the world differently.

Impact 2: Learning to Tolerate Uncertainty in Creative Work

The most uncomfortable phase of the project was design development — the period between completing my research and beginning to draw floor plans. I had extensive notes on what environmental psychology research recommended. But translating those recommendations into a single coherent spatial arrangement involved choices that the research did not uniquely determine. There were many valid designs that could incorporate natural light, acoustic zoning, and biophilic elements. The research could tell me what features a good space should have, but not how, exactly, to arrange them in this particular room with this particular set of constraints.

I found this ambiguity genuinely difficult. I had spent years in school solving problems with right answers — or at least, problems where the grading criteria made it possible to know when you were "correct." Creative design work is not like that. I had to make choices that I could justify but could not prove were optimal, and then commit to them. This experience of productive uncertainty — of making a reasoned decision in the absence of a guaranteed correct answer — connects to what the IB Learner Profile calls being a Risk-Taker: willing to make courageous decisions and to explore uncertainty creatively.

Cii · Evaluating the Product Against Success Criteria

⚠️ Reminder: Evaluate each criterion with your own analysis — not just quoting survey results. Use external evidence as data, then interpret it.
Criterion 1: Every design decision traceable to at least one peer-reviewed source✓ Met
The design report annotation map links 14 distinct design decisions to 9 separate academic or expert sources. My supervisor reviewed the map at Meeting 4 and confirmed the connections were logically coherent. I consider this the most intellectually significant criterion to have met — it means the model is not an aesthetic object but an argument expressed in physical form.
Criterion 2: Minimum 3 distinct evidence-based environmental features✓ Met
The model incorporates 5 distinct evidence-based features: (1) biophilic planting strip along the circulation zone; (2) north-facing glazing simulation for natural light; (3) acoustic panel walls around the quiet focus zone; (4) mixed furniture height options (seated and standing work surfaces); (5) spatial zoning by activity type rather than uniform seating rows. Each is annotated with its research basis in the design report.
Criterion 3: Consistent 1:25 scale, realistic room dimensions✓ Met
The model base represents a room of 15 m × 10 m = 150 m², accommodating a standard class of 30 students at 5.0 m² per student — above the UAE minimum standard of 3.6 m². All furniture is scaled proportionally. This was verified by my design-technology teacher, who measured random elements against the 1:25 ratio.
Criterion 4: ≥60% of student respondents prefer proposed space✓ Met
Survey of 14 students: 11 (79%) rated the proposed space more desirable than their current space. Three students rated the spaces equally. No students preferred the existing space. While I am pleased with this result, I note a significant caveat: students viewed a physical model of an idealised space, not the space itself. The "future desirability" of a model is much easier to rate positively than an actual environment in use. A more rigorous test would have piloted a real redesign in one room and measured before/after usage patterns — something outside the scope of this project but worth investigating if the proposal were ever to be implemented.
Criterion 5: Materials cost ≤ AED 150✓ Met
Total materials cost: AED 118. Cost breakdown in Appendix D. I was able to source foam board from a local stationery supplier at lower cost than the online price by visiting in person.
Criterion 6: Model structurally stable through transport~ Partially met
The main structure arrived at the exhibition intact. However, two miniature planting elements (representing the biophilic strip) detached during transport and had to be re-fixed on site. This was a construction quality failure — I had used insufficient adhesive on these smaller elements. A revised model would use a stronger adhesive (Araldite two-part epoxy rather than PVA) for all decorative elements and would conduct a dedicated transport test before the exhibition day.
✓ Strong Evaluation
  • Criterion 4 includes a genuine self-critical caveat about the survey's limitations
  • Criterion 6 is honest about partial failure — not covered up or minimised
  • Specific solutions for improvement are named (Araldite, transport test)
  • Each evaluation goes beyond "yes or no" to explain the significance
✗ Weak Evaluation
  • "Everyone liked the model" — no evidence or connection to criteria
  • Claiming all criteria "fully met" without evidence
  • Quoting survey percentages without interpreting their limitations
  • Not addressing the criterion where things went wrong

What Distinguishes Quality Work in This Category

✓ Key Quality Indicators
  • Physical product demonstrates and applies the learning — not just illustrates it
  • Design decisions in the model link explicitly to research evidence
  • Criterion C includes a genuine caveat about evaluation methodology (survey limitation acknowledged)
  • Honest reflection on partial product failure (Criterion 6) with specific improvement plan
✗ What Loses Marks
  • Model presented as creative work with no research basis
  • Criteria like "the model should look nice" — aesthetic, not measurable or research-based
  • Ci focused on how much you enjoyed the project rather than what you learned about yourself
  • Cii that just says "students liked it" without addressing each criterion with evidence
