Overview

Introduction & Personal Interest

I grew up in a family that had moved between three countries before I was twelve. In one of them, a period of restricted water access lasted three weeks. I remember the water tank on the roof, the scheduled filling times, the careful rationing my mother organised at mealtimes. When I arrived in the UAE and saw sprinklers running in the midday heat, or read that the UAE has one of the highest per-capita water consumption rates in the world, I experienced a kind of cognitive dissonance I could not explain. How could a country in one of the most water-scarce regions on Earth be simultaneously one of the highest consumers of water per person?

This question was the seed of my project. Through initial research, I discovered that the UAE relies almost entirely on energy-intensive desalination for its freshwater supply, making it one of the most vulnerable countries in the world to the dual pressures of water scarcity and energy cost. I also discovered, however, that individual and institutional behaviour change — not just infrastructure — is identified in the literature as one of the most cost-effective short-term responses to water stress.

This brought me to my learning goal: to develop an understanding of how behaviour-change communication (BCC) theory can be used to design materials that shift awareness and habits around water conservation in adolescent populations — and to apply that understanding to produce a physical awareness campaign for secondary school students.

Key distinction for markers: The learning goal is about behaviour-change communication theory and how it applies to environmental issues. The product goal is producing the campaign materials. Students who write "my goal was to make a water conservation campaign" have merged product and learning — one of the most common reasons for Criterion A marks being withheld.

Planning the Personal Project

Criterion A: 8 marks total (Ai + Aii + Aiii)

Ai · Learning Goal & Global Context

Learning Goal

My learning goal is to understand how behaviour-change communication (BCC) theory — including models such as the Health Belief Model and the Stages of Change framework adapted for environmental behaviour — can be applied to design materials that measurably shift awareness and self-reported intention to act on water conservation issues in a secondary school student population in the UAE.

Specifically, I wanted to understand: (1) what makes environmental communication effective versus ineffective with adolescent audiences; (2) whether fear-based or positive messaging produces better attitude change in this population; (3) how visual design choices (colour, typography, imagery) interact with message content to produce or undermine credibility and engagement.

Global Context: Globalization & Sustainability

I selected Globalization and Sustainability, with a focus on environmental stewardship at the local level as a response to global resource stress. Water scarcity is a global crisis, but its experience is radically unequal: the UAE's geography and economy have insulated its residents from immediate deprivation while simultaneously producing some of the world's highest per-capita consumption. My project explores the tension between local abundance (perceived) and global scarcity (real) — and asks whether communication can bridge that gap in a school community made up of students from over 70 countries, many of whom have very different lived experiences of water access.

Aii · Intended Product & Success Criteria

Intended Product

My intended product is a physical water conservation awareness campaign, consisting of: (1) six A2 printed posters designed using BCC principles (two targeting knowledge, two targeting attitude, two targeting intention/habit); (2) a printed 8-page information booklet (A5, saddle-stitched) with UAE-specific water facts and practical behaviour-change tips; (3) a classroom pledge card (A6, printed on card stock), which students sign to commit to one specific water-conservation behaviour change. All materials are designed in a cohesive visual system — consistent colour palette, typography, and iconography — and are physically printed and displayed in school.

Aiii · Action Plan

Weeks 1–2
Research — BCC Theory & UAE Water Context

Read primary sources on behaviour-change communication, effective environmental messaging for adolescents, and UAE-specific water data. Apply CRAAP test to all sources. Conduct baseline student knowledge survey to understand starting point.

Week 3
Message Mapping — BCC Framework to Content

Map the 6 posters to BCC message types. Draft key messages for each. Identify BCC technique for each. Get supervisor feedback on message strategy before designing.

Weeks 4–5
Visual Design — Style Guide & Poster Mockups

Develop visual system: colour palette (inspired by water/desert duality of UAE), typography, iconography. Design all 6 posters and booklet layout. Get DT teacher feedback on readability and visual hierarchy.

Week 6
Readability Check & Revision

Run Flesch-Kincaid test on booklet text. Revise sentences where FK grade falls above 9. Re-test. Finalise all files for print.

Week 7
Printing & Physical Assembly

Print posters, booklets, and pledge cards. Mount posters. Saddle-stitch booklets. Verify physical quality of all materials before display.

Weeks 8–9
Campaign Display & Data Collection

Display posters. Distribute booklets and pledge cards to target group. Collect pledges. Run post-survey after 2 weeks of display.

Weeks 10–12
Report Writing & Evaluation

Analyse before/after survey data. Calculate shift against success criteria. Write full PP report (Criteria A, B, C). Final supervisor meeting and submission.

Criterion B — Applying Skills

Applying ATL Skills

Criterion B: 8 marks total (Bi + Bii)

Bi · ATL Skills Applied to the Learning Goal

Research Skills — Distinguishing Effective from Ineffective Environmental Messaging

My most significant research finding contradicted my initial assumption. I had assumed that presenting alarming statistics about water scarcity — images of dried riverbeds, data on desalination costs — would be the most effective approach for a conservation campaign. The literature suggests the opposite for adolescent audiences. Moser and Dilling (2011) found that high-fear environmental messages often produce "psychological distancing" in adolescents: rather than motivating action, they trigger a feeling of helplessness that reduces engagement. Instead, research on effective environmental BCC with young people (Slater, 2006; Steg and Vlek, 2009) suggests that positive identity affirmation ("students like you are making a difference") and specific, achievable behaviour suggestions (rather than global problem framing) produce stronger intention-to-act scores. This finding directly changed my message strategy: I moved from alarm-based to identity-based and solution-focused messaging, with the fear-based framing appearing only in contextual information (the booklet) rather than the primary calls to action (the posters).

Communication Skills — Adapting Academic Content for a Non-Specialist Audience

The booklet required me to translate academic research into language appropriate for a 13–17 year old reader with no background in environmental science or communication theory. This required more skill than I had anticipated. My first draft of the section on desalination was written at FK Grade 11.2 — too complex. I learned that long noun phrases ("the energy-intensive process of reverse osmosis desalination") were the primary source of difficulty, and replaced them with shorter analogies ("desalination is like filtering seawater to make it drinkable — a process that takes as much energy as running a large air conditioner all day"). After revision, the FK grade fell to 8.3, within my target range. This process of audience analysis and iterative revision is, I now recognise, a form of Communication skill in the ATL framework: understanding my reader's needs and rewriting until the text serves them.

Bii · ATL Skills Applied to Creating the Product

Creative Thinking — Solving the Colour Paradox

An early design problem: my campaign's visual identity needed to communicate "water" (blue) and "UAE environment" (desert, sand, gold/orange) — but blue and orange are high-contrast complementary colours that can feel aggressive or commercial rather than calm and informative, if not handled carefully. I experimented with five different palette combinations before settling on a deep ocean teal (dark enough to work as a text background) and a soft sand tone (warm but not orange) as the secondary. The design teacher who reviewed my prototypes pointed out that this palette worked specifically because the teal read as "water depth" rather than "bright blue," which felt more serious and less like a corporate logo. Making a considered creative decision — and being able to articulate why it worked — was a thinking skill I had not expected the project to require.

Self-Management — Planning for Print Production Constraints

I designed the campaign materials in a screen-first environment (laptop with RGB colour), but the final product required print (CMYK). When I received the first print proof of Poster 1, the teal appeared significantly greener and less saturated than on screen — a known issue with RGB-to-CMYK colour conversion that I had not anticipated. I had to learn about colour profile management mid-project, convert my files from RGB to CMYK in my design software, and adjust the colour values to approximate the intended appearance in print. I also learned to request a single test print before producing all six posters — a professional practice known as "proof checking" that I would not have thought to do without this experience. Managing this unexpected technical constraint without derailing the overall timeline was a self-management challenge that required both problem-solving and flexible scheduling.

Criterion C — Reflecting

Reflecting on Learning & Product

Criterion C: 8 marks total (Ci + Cii)

Ci · Impact on Learning & Personal Development

Impact 1: Learning Changes What I See, Not Just What I Know

Before this project, I experienced advertising and public communication as a largely unconscious background to daily life — posters I passed without noticing, messages that influenced me without my awareness. Studying BCC theory changed that. I now read public communication analytically: when I see a road-safety campaign poster, I can identify which technique it is using (fear appeal? social norm? identity affirmation?). When I see an environmental charity advertisement using photographs of suffering animals, I can connect it to the literature on emotional appeals — and I can also predict, based on Moser and Dilling's findings, why it might actually be less effective at generating donations than an image of people successfully solving the problem.

This analytical lens did not exist for me before this project. It is not a piece of knowledge I added — it is a permanent change in how I receive and interpret a category of human communication. The IB Learner Profile describes a Knowledgeable learner as one who can connect knowledge to the real world with conceptual understanding; this project gave me a concept (BCC theory) that I now apply automatically to real-world situations I encounter every day.

Impact 2: The Discomfort of Simplification

The most intellectually uncomfortable moment of the project came when I was writing the knowledge-based posters. My research showed that effective BCC messages are simple, specific, and single-issue: one fact, one behaviour, one ask. But the water crisis in the UAE is not simple. It involves desalination economics, groundwater depletion, agricultural water use, geopolitical vulnerability, and climate change. Reducing this to "Turn off the tap while brushing your teeth" felt dishonest — almost insulting to the complexity of the problem.

I discussed this tension with my supervisor, who pointed me to a passage in Rogers (2003) on the "diffusion of innovations" — the idea that effective communication must meet people where they are, not where the communicator thinks they should be. A message that captures the full complexity of a problem is, for most audiences, a message that does nothing. I came to accept, reluctantly, that the responsible communication choice was simplification — and to address the complexity in the booklet, where a reader who wanted more depth could find it. This was an uncomfortable lesson: that doing good communication sometimes means letting go of intellectual rigour at the surface level, in service of a goal that the intellectual rigour, alone, could not achieve.

Cii · Evaluating the Product Against Success Criteria

Reminder: Evaluate honestly. If a criterion was not met, say so clearly and explain why — and what you would change.
Criterion 1: BCC technique traceable for each poster✓ Met
The annotation guide (Appendix C) maps each poster to a specific BCC technique: Posters 1–2 use "social normalisation" framing ("In the UAE, students who…"); Posters 3–4 use "loss aversion" framing for attitude change ("Every time you…, a litre of desalination energy is…"); Posters 5–6 use "implementation intention" framing, naming a specific cue, behaviour, and outcome. Each annotation includes the source that describes the technique. Verified by supervisor at Meeting 4.
Criterion 2: ≥15% increase in correct responses (knowledge survey)✓ Met
Pre-survey (n=18): average correct responses 3.2/8 (40%). Post-survey (n=17, one student absent): average correct responses 5.8/8 (73%). Increase of 33 percentage points — well above the 15% threshold. The largest gains were on UAE-specific statistics: the desalination dependency figure (98%) went from 22% correct to 88% correct. The lowest gain was on the "daily per-capita consumption" question, where only 52% were correct post-survey, possibly because the number (550L) is counterintuitively large and harder to retain than a simpler statistic.
Criterion 3: ≥50% of post-survey respondents pledge a behaviour change✓ Met
Of 17 post-survey respondents, 13 (76%) returned a completed pledge card with a specific behaviour listed. The most common pledges were: "turn off tap while brushing teeth" (6), "take showers under 4 minutes" (4), and "report dripping taps in school" (3). I note a significant limitation: signing a pledge card in a school setting may create social desirability bias — students may sign because they feel they should, not because they genuinely intend to change behaviour. A more rigorous evaluation would follow up 4 weeks later to ask whether the pledged behaviour had been maintained; this was outside my project timeline.
Criterion 4: Consistent visual system across all materials✓ Met
The style guide (Appendix C) documents the colour system (3 colours used consistently), typography (Lexend for headings and body text, chosen for accessibility and readability), and icon set (custom line icons in consistent stroke weight). The design-technology teacher confirmed that all six posters and the booklet adhere to the style guide. One deviation: the pledge card uses a slightly different layout than the booklet, but the DT teacher confirmed this was contextually appropriate (different format, different purpose) rather than a consistency failure.
Criterion 5: Production cost ≤ AED 200~ Partially met
Total: AED 214. This exceeded the target by AED 14 (7%) due to an unanticipated cost: the first print run of two posters required reprinting after the colour-management correction (RGB-to-CMYK conversion), and I paid for both prints. The reprinting cost AED 22, which I had not budgeted for. Had I conducted a proof check before printing all materials, I would have avoided this cost and met the budget criterion. This was a planning failure I would correct in a future project by always including a 10–15% contingency budget and proof-checking before full print runs.
Criterion 6: Booklet readability FK Grade 7–9✓ Met
Final FK grade after revision: 8.1. Test run via Microsoft Word's built-in readability tool on the 1,247-word booklet body text. Screenshot evidence in Appendix E. Initial draft FK grade was 11.2; revision reduced this by simplifying sentence structure, reducing noun phrase complexity, and replacing passive voice constructions with active voice.
