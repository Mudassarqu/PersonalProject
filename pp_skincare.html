Overview

Introduction & Personal Interest

I have always been fascinated by the ingredients list on my skincare products. Growing up, my mother kept a small collection of traditional herbal remedies — rosewater for toning, coconut oil for moisturising, and turmeric paste for blemishes. Watching her blend these natural ingredients made me curious: why do these work? What is the actual chemistry behind plant-based skincare?

This curiosity intensified when I began researching the skincare industry for a school debate on environmental sustainability. I discovered that many commercial products contain synthetic preservatives and chemical emulsifiers that can irritate sensitive skin, and that the cosmetics industry generates significant plastic waste. I wanted to understand whether natural formulation could offer a genuinely effective alternative — not just a marketing claim.

My personal interest, therefore, is the intersection of natural chemistry and sustainable beauty. This interest led directly to my learning goal: I want to learn the science of plant-based cosmetic formulation — understanding emulsification, pH balance, preservative systems, and active botanical ingredients — so that I can create skincare products that are both scientifically sound and genuinely effective.

Why this matters: According to the Global Cosmetics Market Report (2024), the natural skincare sector is growing at 8.1% annually, driven by consumer demand for transparency and sustainability. Understanding the science behind these products equips me with knowledge relevant to chemistry, biology, and environmental science — disciplines I plan to study further.
✓ Good Practice Shown Here
  • Personal interest is specific and authentic, not generic
  • The connection between interest and learning goal is clearly explained
  • The introduction shows why the student wants to learn, not just what they made
✗ Common Student Mistakes
  • Writing only about childhood stories without connecting to a learning goal
  • Making the learning goal simply "learn how to make the product"

Criterion A — Planning

Planning the Personal Project

Criterion A: 8 marks total (Ai + Aii + Aiii)

Ai · Learning Goal & Global Context

Learning Goal

My learning goal is to develop an understanding of the science of plant-based cosmetic formulation, specifically: how natural emulsifiers create stable oil-water emulsions; how pH levels affect skincare efficacy and safety; which botanical active ingredients (such as aloe vera, rosehip oil, and green tea extract) have evidence-based benefits; and how natural preservative systems function to extend shelf life without synthetic chemicals.

This is a distinct learning goal from my product goal. Creating the skincare product is the means by which I will demonstrate and apply my learning — but the knowledge I gain about formulation chemistry is the actual learning outcome.

⚠️ Student Warning: Do not write "My learning goal is to learn how to make a natural skincare product." This confuses the learning goal with the product goal. Your learning goal must be the knowledge or understanding you gain through the project — the "so what?" beyond making the thing.

Global Context: Identities & Relationships

I chose the global context of Identities and Relationships, specifically the exploration of how individuals relate to their own bodies, health, and cultural practices. Skincare is deeply tied to cultural identity — different communities have traditions of plant-based beauty rituals passed down through generations. My project explores how scientific understanding can honour and elevate these cultural practices, helping me understand the relationship between traditional knowledge and modern science.

This context also connects to questions of self-image, health choices, and the ethical responsibilities of consumer culture — all relevant to my own identity as a young person navigating beauty standards and environmental values.

Aii · Intended Product & Success Criteria

Intended Product

My intended product is a set of three natural skincare products: (1) a botanical facial cleanser, (2) a rosehip & aloe vera moisturising serum, and (3) a green tea & kaolin clay face mask. The products will be housed in labelled, recyclable glass jars and accompanied by a written formulation guide explaining the science behind each ingredient. The products are designed for combination to sensitive skin types, appropriate for the 15–25 age group.

Bi · ATL Skills Applied to the Learning Goal

To achieve my learning goal — understanding plant-based cosmetic formulation science — I selected two ATL skills that were most central to this intellectual work:

Research Skills — Information Literacy

I needed to evaluate the scientific validity of sources on botanical ingredients, since there is a great deal of misinformation in the beauty industry. I applied information literacy using the CRAAP test (Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose) to evaluate each source before including it in my research. For example, I initially found a popular website claiming that lemon juice is an effective toner due to its vitamin C content. Applying the CRAAP test, I noted this source had no author credentials, no citations to peer-reviewed research, and an apparent commercial purpose (selling a lemon-based product). I rejected it and instead found Levine et al. (2001) in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology, which demonstrated that citrus juice at undiluted concentrations (pH ~2.0) causes phototoxic skin damage — the opposite of the wellness claim.

This research skill was critical to my learning goal because it enabled me to distinguish evidence-based formulation from marketing-driven claims. Without it, I would have reproduced harmful misinformation in my own products.

Thinking Skills — Critical Thinking

Understanding why certain botanical ingredients are effective required me to move beyond surface-level information and analyse the underlying chemistry. When I researched rosehip oil, I initially recorded "high in vitamin C" as its main benefit — a claim I had seen repeatedly online. However, critical analysis of the primary literature revealed that rosehip oil's vitamin C content is largely destroyed during cold-pressing and is not stable in oil form (Venditti et al., 2020). Its actual benefit comes from its high concentration of linoleic acid (omega-6), which restores the skin barrier. This distinction was not immediately obvious, and recognising it required me to cross-reference multiple peer-reviewed studies.

This critical thinking process transformed my understanding from "rosehip oil has vitamin C" — a false claim — to "rosehip oil strengthens the lipid barrier through essential fatty acids" — a chemically accurate explanation. This correction directly changed my formulation approach and the ingredient claims I made in my product's accompanying guide.

Bii · ATL Skills Applied to Creating the Product

Creating the physical skincare products required a different set of skills from intellectual research. The two ATL skills I applied most significantly in this phase were:

Self-Management Skills — Organisation

Cosmetic formulation requires precise measurement and sequencing. A deviation of even 1–2% in emulsifier concentration can cause emulsion failure. I developed a formulation log — a structured document recording exact ingredient weights (in grams), batch temperatures, mixing times, and testing results for each trial batch. This tool proved essential during Batch 1, when my serum separated after 36 hours. Reviewing my log, I identified that I had dissolved the emulsifier at 68°C rather than the required 75°C, causing incomplete activation. My organisation skills allowed me to trace the root cause and correct it in Batch 2, where the serum remained stable for over 96 hours — exceeding my success criterion of 72 hours.

Communication Skills — Media Literacy

Creating the packaging labels required me to communicate complex scientific information accessibly to a non-specialist audience. This was more difficult than I anticipated. My first draft used ingredient names from the International Nomenclature of Cosmetic Ingredients (INCI) system, which is the global standard — but focus group feedback from my user testers revealed that terms like "Butyrospermum parkii" (shea butter) and "Centella asiatica extract" were confusing. I resolved this by using a dual-label approach: INCI names (required for regulatory compliance) listed first, followed by the common name in brackets. This communication solution demonstrated my understanding of both regulatory requirements and consumer accessibility.

Criterion C — Reflecting

Reflecting on Learning & Product

Criterion C: 8 marks total (Ci + Cii)

Ci · Impact on Learning & Personal Development

The personal project has had two significant impacts on me as a learner. I focus on these two in depth, rather than listing many impacts briefly — because the IB moderator notes that "several brief statements do not together constitute a description or explanation."

Impact 1: The Development of Scientific Scepticism

Before this project, I believed that "natural" automatically meant "safe" and "effective." I had absorbed this assumption from social media, from beauty influencers, and even from my own family's herbal practices. The moment this belief shifted was when I discovered — through primary literature — that lemon juice, one of the most-recommended "natural" skincare ingredients on TikTok, causes phototoxic burns at the concentrations typically advised.

This discovery forced me to confront my own intellectual laziness. I had never questioned whether "natural" claims were supported by evidence. Learning to apply the CRAAP test and then to interrogate the methodology of studies themselves (sample size, control groups, conflict of interest) fundamentally changed how I approach information — not just in skincare, but in all areas. I now habitually ask: "Who says this? What is their evidence? What financial interest do they have?"

This is what the IB Learner Profile calls being an Inquirer: developing skills for inquiry that sustain a love of learning. I did not have this skill at the start of the project. I have it now.

Impact 2: Tolerating and Learning from Failure

My first serum batch failed. After 36 hours, the oil and water phases separated completely. When I opened the jar, I felt a wave of frustration and self-doubt that I did not expect. I had planned this batch carefully. I had followed the formula. Why had it failed?

The process of diagnosing the failure — reviewing my formulation log, re-reading the emulsification literature, identifying the temperature error — taught me something I cannot learn from a textbook: that failure is a precise source of information, not a sign of incompetence. By the time Batch 2 succeeded, I had a much deeper understanding of emulsification chemistry than I would have gained from a straightforward first success.

This connects to the Learner Profile attribute of being a Risk-Taker: I approached uncertainty with determination, and I used the failure to explore rather than to retreat. I believe this experience will have lasting relevance for how I approach challenges in science and in life.

Cii · Evaluating the Product Against Success Criteria

I evaluate my product against each of the six success criteria I established at the start of the project. I balance strengths and limitations, and I support each judgement with specific evidence from the product itself — not solely from survey results.

⚠️ Key Moderator Feedback (June 2026): "Survey data and external opinions can provide useful information, but they do not constitute evaluation unless they are interpreted and used to support the student's own analysis." Your evaluation must be YOUR OWN critical judgement. You can reference what testers said, but you must interpret and analyse it.
Criterion 1: pH between 4.5 and 6.0 ✓ Met Fully achieved
All three products tested within the target pH range: cleanser 5.2, serum 4.8, face mask 5.6. I tested each product three times on separate occasions to account for measurement error from pH strips. The consistency of results (±0.2 variation) confirms the formulas are reliably calibrated. The face mask at pH 5.6 sits at the upper end of the range, which I attribute to the kaolin clay's natural alkalinity. In a future formulation, I would add a small amount of citric acid to bring this down to the midpoint of the range (pH 5.0), which would improve the safety margin for long-term use.
Criterion 2: Emulsion stability — no separation after 72 hours ✓ Met (exceeded) Exceeded criterion
The final serum formulation remained fully stable for 96 hours — 24 hours beyond the target. I attribute this to increasing the emulsifier (BTMS-50) from 3% to 4% in Batch 2, informed by the formulation literature. This improvement was made possible by my detailed formulation log, which allowed me to identify the exact variable that caused Batch 1 failure. Photographs at 24h, 48h, 72h, and 96h intervals are included in Appendix B.
Criterion 3: At least 2 evidence-based botanical actives per product ✓ Met Fully achieved
Each product contains two to three evidence-based botanical actives. The serum contains aloe vera (anti-inflammatory, confirmed by Surjushe et al., 2008) and rosehip oil (skin barrier restoration, confirmed by Valerón-Almazán et al., 2015). The face mask contains green tea extract (antioxidant, confirmed by Katiyar, 2011) and kaolin clay (pore-clearing, confirmed by Moosavi, 2017). This criterion was the most intellectually rigorous to achieve, because I had to critically distinguish between evidence-based ingredients and those with only anecdotal or commercial support.
Criterion 4: Recyclable/biodegradable packaging only ✓ Met Fully achieved
All three products are housed in borosilicate glass jars with aluminium lids, both of which are infinitely recyclable. Labels are printed on recycled kraft paper with water-based ink. I chose not to use plastic pumps, although they would have improved user experience, because they are not currently recyclable in Dubai's recycling infrastructure. This was a deliberate trade-off between aesthetics and the environmental criterion I had set myself — and I believe the criterion was the right priority.
Criterion 5: Average rating ≥ 4/5 from 5 testers ~ Partially met 2 of 3 products met threshold
The cleanser (4.4/5 average) and serum (4.2/5 average) met the threshold. The face mask averaged 3.6/5, falling below the target. Three of five testers commented that the mask had an "earthy, clay-like scent" that they found unpleasant. I had expected this, because kaolin clay has a distinct natural smell, but I had not accounted for how much this would affect the sensory experience score. In a refined version, I would add a small quantity of cosmetic-grade lavender essential oil (under 0.5% to remain within safe use limits) to mask the clay smell, while maintaining the product's natural formulation. This limitation helped me understand that sensory properties are as important as functional properties in product design — a lesson I might not have learned from a textbook.
Criterion 6: Material cost ≤ AED 150 per 50ml batch ✓ Met Fully achieved
Final material costs were: cleanser AED 82, serum AED 118, face mask AED 94 — all well within the AED 150 limit. The most significant cost driver was rosehip oil (AED 38 for 30ml), which I sourced from a local wholesaler rather than a retail store, reducing cost by approximately 40%. The glass jars represented the second-largest cost (AED 18 each), but I considered this necessary to meet criterion 4. A detailed cost breakdown is provided in Appendix C.

Guidance for Students

Summary: What Makes or Breaks a Personal Project

✓ THE 5 THINGS THAT EARN TOP MARKS
  • Clear separation of learning goal vs. product goal — maintained throughout every section
  • Deep ATL coverage on 1–2 skills per strand, with specific examples and evidence linked to claims
  • Product-specific success criteria that are measurable and justified by research
  • Action plan that focuses on product development and explicitly links to success criteria
  • Student-led evaluation that interprets evidence, admits limitations honestly, and suggests genuine improvements
✗ THE 5 MOST COMMON REASONS FOR LOST MARKS
  • Learning goal = "learn how to make [the product]" — this is the product goal, not a learning goal
  • Listing 6+ ATL skills without depth — IB moderators consistently penalise this
  • Generic success criteria copied from a template ("the product should be good quality")
  • Evaluation that only reports survey averages without the student's own interpretation
  • Reflection that lists many impacts briefly instead of exploring 1–2 impacts deeply

"The distinction between the learning goal and the product goal is the single most important concept in the entire Personal Project."

Ask yourself: if you had NOT made the product, what would you still have learned? That learning is your learning goal. The product is simply the means by which you demonstrate and apply it.
