Overview

Introduction & Personal Interest

I have played football since the age of eight. Over the years, I have trained with three different clubs and noticed something that troubled me: the training methods used for 12–16 year olds were almost identical to those used for adults. We ran the same drills, lifted the same relative loads, and recovered on the same schedules. Yet I had heard coaches and sports science teachers mention that adolescent bodies respond differently to training — that young athletes face specific risks, including growth plate injuries and overtraining syndrome, that are far less common in adults.

This observation sparked a question that I could not let go of: What does sports science actually say about training adolescents? I wanted to understand the physiological principles behind youth athletic development — not just the exercises, but the reasoning: why certain training methods are appropriate or harmful for young bodies, how rest and recovery function biologically, and how these principles should inform the design of a training programme for my age group.

My personal interest therefore lies at the intersection of sports performance and youth physiology. This led directly to my learning goal: to develop a scientifically grounded understanding of youth athletic development principles that I could apply to create a practical, evidence-based training manual for athletes aged 12–16.

Why this matters: According to the World Health Organisation (2022), physical inactivity among adolescents is a global health crisis, with 81% of school-aged children failing to meet recommended activity levels. At the same time, inappropriate training leads to increasing rates of youth sports injuries. Understanding the evidence base behind safe, effective youth training is both personally meaningful and socially relevant.

Criterion A — Planning

Planning the Personal Project

Criterion A: 8 marks total (Ai + Aii + Aiii)

Ai · Learning Goal & Global Context

Learning Goal

My learning goal is to understand the key principles of youth athletic development from a sports science perspective, specifically: the physiological differences between adolescent and adult training responses; the concept of Long-Term Athlete Development (LTAD); the role of rest, recovery, and periodisation in preventing overtraining; and how to design a training programme that respects growth plate safety while building speed, strength, and endurance progressively.

This learning goal is distinct from my product goal. Creating the training manual is how I will demonstrate and apply this learning — but the scientific understanding I develop is the actual learning outcome of the project.

⚠️ Key Warning: Do not write "My learning goal is to learn how to write a training manual." That is the product goal. The learning goal must describe the knowledge or understanding you gain — the "so what?" that remains even if the product were never made.

Global Context: Identities & Relationships

I selected the global context of Identities and Relationships, specifically the exploration of how physical activity, sport, and the body shape personal identity during adolescence. Sport plays a significant role in how many young people understand themselves — their confidence, their peer relationships, and their sense of achievement. By creating a training manual grounded in science rather than inherited adult practices, I aim to contribute to a healthier relationship between young athletes and their own physical development.

This global context also connects to questions of wellbeing, the ethics of coaching young people, and the responsibility coaches and athletes have to train safely — themes that are personally meaningful given my years of experience in competitive football.

Aii · Intended Product & Success Criteria

Intended Product

My intended product is a printed and bound Youth Athlete Training Manual — a professionally designed, A5-format guide of approximately 24–30 pages, aimed at athletes aged 12–16 and their coaches. The manual includes: a scientific introduction to youth physiology; a 12-week progressive training programme structured around the LTAD framework; illustrated exercise descriptions with correct form guidance; recovery and nutrition principles; and a weekly self-monitoring log. The manual will be produced in physical print form as a finished, usable resource, displayed at the project exhibition alongside a sample training session demonstration.

Criterion B — Applying Skills

Applying ATL Skills

Criterion B: 8 marks total (Bi + Bii)
IB Moderator Insight: "More successful reports focused on one or two carefully selected ATL skills, providing richer explanations supported by specific, clearly referenced evidence." Go deep on 1–2 skills per strand — do not list 6+ skills briefly.

Bi · ATL Skills Applied to the Learning Goal

To develop a genuine understanding of youth athletic development science, two ATL skills were central to my learning process:

Research Skills — Critical Thinking about Evidence

Much of the publicly available content on youth training — websites, YouTube channels, club coaching manuals — is based on adult sports science transferred to younger athletes without modification. To develop genuine understanding, I had to go beyond these sources and read primary sports science literature on youth physiology. A key moment came when I found three popular coaching websites recommending 1-repetition maximum (1RM) testing for adolescents to establish strength baselines. Reading Caine et al. (2006) in the British Journal of Sports Medicine, I discovered that maximum-load testing in athletes with open growth plates carries a documented risk of epiphyseal fracture — a growth plate injury that can permanently damage bone development. This was directly contradicted by the popular guidance.

This experience transformed my understanding of how to distinguish evidence-based practice from culturally inherited coaching habit. It also shaped Success Criterion 1 of my product — the prohibition of maximum-load strength training is not a personal preference but a conclusion drawn from primary sports science research.

Thinking Skills — Transfer of Knowledge

Understanding the Long-Term Athlete Development (LTAD) framework required me to translate abstract physiological concepts into practical programme design decisions. For example, the research on periodisation (Kraemer & Ratamess, 2004) explains how training load must fluctuate to avoid adaptation plateaus — but the academic presentation assumes adult athletic contexts. I had to critically adapt this principle for a 12–16 year old audience: substituting percentage of 1RM (inappropriate for youth) with Rate of Perceived Exertion (RPE) as the load measurement tool, and reducing overall training volume relative to the adult models by approximately 30%, in line with Balyi et al.'s (2013) LTAD guidance.

This transfer of academic knowledge into age-appropriate application was the most intellectually demanding part of my learning. It required me to hold two bodies of knowledge simultaneously — the general principle and its youth-specific modification — and make reasoned adaptations rather than simple translations.

Bii · ATL Skills Applied to Creating the Product

Communication Skills — Writing for a Specific Audience

Writing a training manual for 12-year-olds requires a fundamentally different voice from writing an academic report. My first draft of the physiology chapter was flagged in readability testing as "too textbook-like" by two of my three young testers. I measured this using the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level formula, which produced a score of Grade 10 — two grade levels above my target audience. I systematically revised the chapter: replacing passive voice with active voice, replacing Latin-root words with Anglo-Saxon equivalents ("insufficient" became "not enough", "approximately" became "about"), and breaking long paragraphs into shorter units with subheadings. After revision, the Flesch-Kincaid score dropped to Grade 7.5, and all three re-testers reported understanding the content without difficulty.

Self-Management Skills — Reflection in Action

Creating the 12-week programme required me to regularly revise my own plans as I learned more. I kept a project journal in which I noted each decision point: when I changed the Week 4 plyometric volume after learning it exceeded LTAD guidelines, or when I restructured the recovery chapter after realising I had included sleep science but omitted nutrition timing — a gap flagged by my PE teacher in the Week 6 review. The journal allowed me to track my own reasoning across the project, making my decision-making visible and reviewable. This habit of recording rationale — not just actions — is something I intend to continue in future projects and in my academic study.

Criterion C — Reflecting

Reflecting on Learning & Product

Criterion C: 8 marks total (Ci + Cii)

Ci · Impact on Learning & Personal Development

I focus on two significant impacts from this project, exploring each in depth rather than listing many briefly.

Impact 1: Questioning What I Thought I Already Knew

I entered this project believing I understood youth training because I had been doing it for seven years. What I actually had was seven years of accumulated habit that I had never questioned. The moment this belief was challenged was when I read the Caine et al. (2006) study on adolescent sports injuries and realised that exercises I had been performing routinely — including heavy back squats at maximum load, which my club coaches had introduced at age 14 — carried documented risks that my coaches had apparently not considered, or had not known about.

This was an uncomfortable discovery. It required me to hold two contradictory ideas simultaneously: that my coaches meant well and had genuine expertise, and that their practice in this specific area was not aligned with current sports science. Processing this discomfort — not dismissing it, and not catastrophising it — was itself a form of learning. I became what the IB Learner Profile calls Reflective: able to thoughtfully consider the evidence for my own practices and honestly assess where they needed to change.

I now approach received wisdom — in sport and beyond — as something to verify rather than simply accept. This shift in intellectual habit feels like one of the most durable outcomes of the entire project.

Impact 2: Understanding the Gap Between Knowing and Communicating

Halfway through drafting the manual, I realised I was writing for myself — not for a 12-year-old. My chapter on periodisation was accurate but impenetrable for its target audience. The readability test confirmed this. Revising it forced me to confront a gap I had not previously noticed: understanding something deeply and being able to explain it clearly to someone else are entirely different skills.

This connected to an insight from my research: the most effective coaches are not always those with the most technical knowledge, but those who can translate that knowledge into language and tasks their athletes can actually use. The process of simplifying without distorting — cutting jargon while preserving accuracy — was far more intellectually demanding than I had expected. I now understand why good communication is listed as a distinct ATL category: it is not a soft skill but a rigorous discipline in itself. This connects to the Learner Profile attribute of being a Communicator.

Cii · Evaluating the Product Against Success Criteria

⚠️ Key Moderator Reminder: Evaluation must be your own critical analysis — not a summary of what testers said. Use external feedback as evidence, then interpret it. Always address criteria that were not fully met.
Criterion 1: LTAD-compliant training loads — no maximum-load strength training ✓ Met Fully achieved
The programme uses RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion) as its load metric throughout, with no reference to 1RM testing or maximum-load protocols. This was verified by my PE teacher, who confirmed alignment with LTAD "Train to Train" stage guidelines. I consider this the most important criterion because it is the one with direct safety implications — a training manual that recommended inappropriate loads could cause real harm to real young athletes.
Criterion 2: Minimum 3 evidence-based recovery strategies with physiological reasoning ✓ Met Fully achieved
The manual includes five recovery strategies: sleep hygiene (with reference to Kellmann et al., 2018), active recovery protocols, nutrition timing, cold-water immersion cautions, and psychological recovery through mindfulness. Each includes a one-paragraph explanation of the physiological mechanism — not just "do this," but "this works because…" My PE teacher commented in the Week 6 review that this was stronger than most adult-oriented coaching resources she had encountered. I interpret this as evidence that the criterion was met and the content approach was appropriate.
Criterion 3: Readable by a 12-year-old without adult assistance ~ Partially met Met for 7 of 8 chapters
Seven of the eight manual chapters achieved a Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level of 7.5 or below after revision. The exception was the nutrition timing chapter, which remained at Grade 8.2 due to the technical vocabulary required (glycogen replenishment, protein synthesis). Two of three testers understood it with minor difficulty; one tester required adult help for two specific paragraphs. I added a glossary box to this chapter defining the technical terms, which I believe partially resolves the issue — but I acknowledge the chapter did not fully meet the criterion. In a revised edition, I would restructure this chapter to introduce technical terms through analogy before using them in context.
Criterion 4: All exercises include diagram + listed common mistake ✓ Met Fully achieved
All 24 exercises in the library include a hand-drawn diagram (photographed and embedded digitally), a step-by-step instruction, and a "Common Mistake" callout box. Creating the diagrams was more time-consuming than planned — each took approximately 45 minutes to draw and photograph — but I am satisfied that the visual consistency across the exercise library gives the manual a professional quality that matches its content.
Criterion 5: Progressive overload — measurable increase across 4-week blocks ✓ Met Fully achieved
Block 1 (Weeks 1–4) establishes movement patterns at RPE 6–7. Block 2 (Weeks 5–8) increases volume by 20% and introduces plyometric complexity. Block 3 (Weeks 9–12) reduces volume by 15% (deload week 11) and increases intensity to RPE 7–8. This design follows the undulating periodisation model adapted from Kraemer & Ratamess (2004) for the youth context. The progression tables are documented in Appendix C.
Criterion 6: Production cost ≤ AED 80 per copy ✓ Met Fully achieved
Final cost per copy: AED 62 (design software subscription: AED 18; A5 colour printing, 28 pages: AED 31; spiral binding: AED 13). This was below the AED 80 threshold by 22%. I achieved this by using a free student design tool for the layout and by obtaining three printing quotes, selecting the most competitive. The cost breakdown is in Appendix D.
