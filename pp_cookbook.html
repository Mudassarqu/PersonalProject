Overview

Introduction & Personal Interest

I was eleven years old when I watched my grandmother demonstrate a dish — hands moving from memory, no measuring, no written instructions — while narrating in a language my mother only half-remembered and I barely understood at all. My grandmother died the following year. The recipe was not written down anywhere. I have never been able to reproduce it exactly, and now there is no one I can ask.

When I came to a school with students from over 60 nationalities, I realised my experience was not unusual. Many of my classmates described dishes, preparation techniques, and food traditions that existed only in living memory — in grandparents, aunts, parents who had emigrated and adapted — and were not preserved in any written form. This struck me as a genuine loss: not just personal, but anthropological. Food, as the scholar Carole Counihan (1999) argues, is one of the most powerful carriers of cultural identity and historical memory. When a dish is lost, something of the culture that produced it is lost too.

This became my learning goal: to develop an understanding of oral history methodology and the anthropology of food — how scholars collect, interpret, and present foodways as cultural documents — and to apply that understanding to produce a physical cookbook that preserves the culinary heritage of families from my school community, with each recipe contextualised not just as instructions but as a cultural artefact.

A note on this topic: A cookbook Personal Project risks becoming simply a compilation of family recipes with no intellectual framework. The learning goal here — oral history methodology + anthropology of food — is what elevates the project. The product is a cookbook; the learning is how to treat food as cultural evidence.

Criterion A — Planning

Planning the Personal Project

Criterion A: 8 marks total (Ai + Aii + Aiii)

Ai · Learning Goal & Global Context

Learning Goal

My learning goal is to understand: (1) the principles of oral history methodology — how to conduct ethical, rigorous interviews for cultural documentation purposes; (2) the academic discipline of food anthropology — how scholars use food practices as evidence of cultural identity, migration history, and community belonging; (3) how these two disciplines together can be used to design a cookbook that functions as a cultural document rather than a mere instruction set.

I am deliberately separating this from my product goal, which is to produce the physical cookbook. The learning goal would be valuable whether or not the cookbook existed: I would come away with a richer understanding of how oral history is conducted, of what makes a piece of cultural documentation rigorous versus superficial, and of how the scholarly treatment of everyday practices (food, dress, music) can illuminate aspects of human experience that conventional history overlooks.

Global Context: Personal & Cultural Expression

I selected Personal and Cultural Expression, exploring the question: how do communities express and preserve cultural identity through food, and what happens to that identity when families migrate? The school community that contributed recipes to this cookbook represents a case study in cultural expression under conditions of diaspora. Every recipe submitted had adapted in some way — to available ingredients in the UAE, to the preferences of a second generation, to the nutritional knowledge of a parent who had read different information than their own parents had access to. These adaptations are not corruptions of authentic culture; they are the living record of culture responding to new conditions. The cookbook documents both what the dish was and what it has become, treating adaptation as evidence rather than error.

Aii · Intended Product & Success Criteria

Intended Product

My intended product is a physical, professionally printed and perfect-bound cookbook (A5 format, minimum 40 pages, printed on 120gsm matte paper with a laminated card cover), containing: (1) a minimum of 12 recipes from 12 different family backgrounds, each with a photographic record of the dish; (2) for each recipe, a cultural context section (150–250 words) drawing on interview data and academic sources to contextualise the dish's origins, significance, and adaptation; (3) a foreword that discusses the oral history methodology used in collecting the recipes; (4) a printed contributor acknowledgements page. The cookbook will be printed in a limited edition of 5 copies for distribution to contributing families, plus one copy for school exhibition.

Sample Recipe Layout (from the cookbook)

01
Mansaf الم‌نسف
Jordan | Al-Karak region

Ingredients (serves 6)

  • 1kg lamb shoulder, bone-in
  • 500g jameed (dried fermented yoghurt)
  • 500g long-grain rice
  • 2 tbsp samneh (clarified butter)
  • Pine nuts and almonds to garnish
  • Flatbread (markook) to serve

Cultural Notes

[Contributor: Al-Karak family, 3rd generation]

Method (contributor version)

  1. Dissolve jameed in warm water overnight until smooth
  2. Simmer lamb in salted water until tender, reserve stock
  3. Cook rice in lamb stock with samneh
  4. Bring jameed sauce to simmer with stock, add lamb
  5. Layer rice on flatbread, add lamb, pour sauce over
  6. Garnish with toasted pine nuts and almonds
Oral History Note: The contributor's family has made Mansaf for weddings and funerals for four generations. In the UAE context, jameed is sourced from a specialist supplier in Satwa, Dubai — an adaptation made necessary by local availability. The dish remains central to the family's identity: "When we make Mansaf, everyone comes home," the contributor told me (Interview Transcript 3, Appendix B).

Aiii · Action Plan

Weeks 1–2
Research — Oral History Methodology & Food Anthropology

Read primary academic sources on oral history methodology (interviewing techniques, ethical principles, transcription standards) and food anthropology (Counihan; Mintz; Appadurai). Design interview protocol and consent form with supervisor review.

Weeks 3–5
Primary Research — Family Interviews

Conduct minimum 10 family interviews (30–45 minutes each). Transcribe or summarise each. Photograph families cooking or presenting the dish. Collect recipe details and any written family records. Apply oral history methodology (probing questions, context-building, follow-up).

Weeks 6–7
Cultural Context Writing

Write 150–250 word cultural context section for each recipe, drawing on interview data and academic sources. Each section must cite at least one academic source on the dish or its cultural tradition. Send context drafts back to families for accuracy check.

Week 8
Food Photography

Photograph all 12 dishes (either in families' homes during cooking, or in school food tech kitchen if families are unable to host). Apply basic food photography principles (natural light, plating, angle).

Weeks 9–10
Design, Layout & Print Preparation

Design cookbook layout (A5, grid system, consistent typography). Design cover. Prepare files for professional print. Proof read all content. Order test copy before final print run.

Weeks 11–12
Final Print, Report Writing & Exhibition

Print and bind final copies. Distribute to contributing families; collect feedback (Criterion 6). Write PP report (Criteria A, B, C). Final supervisor meeting. Prepare exhibition display.

Criterion B — Applying Skills

Applying ATL Skills

Criterion B: 8 marks total (Bi + Bii)

Bi · ATL Skills Applied to the Learning Goal

Research Skills — Applying Oral History Methodology

Before this project, I thought of interviewing as simply asking people questions and writing down their answers. Reading Yow (2005) on oral history methodology revealed that what sounds like a simple conversation is actually a highly structured research method with established ethical protocols, question design principles, and transcription standards. The most important lesson was the difference between a directive interview (where the interviewer's questions shape the narrative) and a semi-structured open interview (where the participant's own narrative takes precedence). For my first two interviews, I found myself interrupting with follow-up questions at the wrong moments, closing off more interesting tangents to ask my pre-planned questions. By Interview 4, I had learned to stay with a participant's direction and use my planned questions only as a scaffold rather than a script. The difference in the richness of data collected between early and later interviews is visible in the transcripts (Appendix B).

Thinking Skills — Interpreting Food as Cultural Evidence

The most intellectually stimulating aspect of the research was learning to read recipes and cooking practices as evidence — not just descriptions of how to make something, but as records of migration, adaptation, economic constraint, and cultural negotiation. For example: the Mansaf recipe contributed by one family uses jameed sourced from a Dubai supplier, where the original Jordanian preparation would use locally produced jameed. This substitution is not a deviation; it is evidence of diaspora. As Mintz (1996) argues, "the history of food is inseparable from the history of people who ate it." Learning to think this way — to treat a substituted ingredient as a data point rather than a detail — was a genuinely new form of thinking for me, and one I did not possess before this project.

Bii · ATL Skills Applied to Creating the Product

Social Skills — Navigating Cultural Sensitivity in Research

Two incidents tested my social skills during the data collection phase. In the first, a family declined to share their recipe in writing, explaining that it was traditionally passed down orally only — putting it in a printed cookbook felt wrong to them. Rather than trying to persuade them, I thanked them, adjusted my participant count, and found an additional contributor. This was the ethically correct response; Oral History Association guidelines (2009) are clear that participant withdrawal at any stage is their right and must be honoured without challenge. In the second incident, one family's cultural context review identified factual errors in my draft — I had attributed a dish to the "wrong" regional identity within a country (conflating a national dish with a specific regional one). Receiving this correction without defensiveness, and revising the text accordingly, was a social skill as much as a research skill.

Communication Skills — Writing for Multiple Audiences

The cookbook had to work for two distinct audiences simultaneously: the contributing families (who needed to recognise their dishes accurately and feel their culture was represented with respect) and a general reader from any background (who might be encountering the dish for the first time). These two demands sometimes conflict. A level of cultural specificity that a Jordanian reader would take for granted ("this is a dish of celebration in the Karak tradition") might be opaque to a reader unfamiliar with Jordanian regional geography. I developed a "dual-layer" writing approach for the context sections: one sentence for cultural insiders (naming the specific tradition), one sentence of general contextualisation for outsiders ("the Karak region of southern Jordan has historically been a centre of Bedouin culture"). Learning to write for multiple audiences without condescending to either was a communication skill I will use far beyond this project.

Criterion C — Reflecting

Reflecting on Learning & Product

Criterion C: 8 marks total (Ci + Cii)

Ci · Impact on Learning & Personal Development

Impact 1: The Practice of Ethical Listening

The deepest learning of this project was not academic; it was relational. Oral history methodology, as Yow (2005) frames it, is fundamentally about creating conditions for another person's story to be heard accurately — not shaped, not simplified, not interrupted. Practising this for 12 interviews over six weeks changed something in how I listen outside the project context too. I notice when I am formulating my response before someone has finished speaking. I notice when I am steering a conversation toward what I want to know rather than what the other person is able or willing to share. I notice when a silence that makes me uncomfortable is actually productive — when the person I am interviewing is reaching for something more considered, and my instinct to fill the space would have prevented them from finding it.

These are not skills that appear on a CV or a grade report. But they are, I believe, among the most practically useful things I have developed in five years of secondary school. The IB Learner Profile describes an Open-Minded learner as one who "seek[s] and evaluate[s] a range of points of view." Oral history methodology is, I now think, the most structured practice of open-mindedness that exists.

Impact 2: Realising What I Did Not Know About My Own Heritage

I entered this project expecting to learn about other people's cultures. I did not expect to discover how much I did not know about my own. When I came to write the cultural context section for the dish contributed by my own family, I found myself unable to answer questions I had never thought to ask. I knew the recipe; I did not know its origin, its regional significance, or the circumstances in which my family had adapted it over three generations of migration. The academic framework I had built to analyse everyone else's recipes exposed the gaps in my own cultural knowledge.

I interviewed my own mother for the first time using the oral history protocol — structured questions, active listening, not interrupting. She told me things she had never mentioned in ordinary conversation: details about my grandmother's cooking during a period of economic hardship; adaptations she made when she arrived in a new country and certain ingredients were unavailable; the moment she realised she was making her mother's dish, but not her grandmother's. This conversation, generated by a research methodology I had never previously encountered, felt like recovering something I had not known was lost.

Cii · Evaluating the Product Against Success Criteria

Criterion 1: ≥12 recipes from ≥12 different family/cultural backgrounds✓ Met
Final cookbook contains 14 recipes from 14 different contributor families representing: Jordan, India (Tamil Nadu), Lebanon, Philippines, Pakistan, Kenya, Egypt, Nigeria, Iran, South Korea, Sri Lanka, Brazil, Ethiopia, and Ukraine. Two additional contributors were recruited after one family withdrew (Criterion 3 reference), bringing the total above the minimum.
Criterion 2: Cultural context section (150–250 words, ≥1 academic citation) per recipe~ Mostly met
12 of 14 context sections are fully within the 150–250 word range and include at least one academic citation. Two sections (the Ethiopian recipe and the Ukrainian recipe) were more difficult to contextualise academically: the specific dish from Ethiopia was not covered in sources I could access within my timeline, and the academic literature on Ukrainian foodways in English is limited. For these two sections, I cited reputable cultural organisation websites (UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage entry and a peer-reviewed geography journal article respectively) rather than food anthropology texts. I consider this a partial achievement of the criterion — the contextualisation is present and credible, but at a lower academic standard than the other 12 sections.
Criterion 3: ≥10 interviews with documented ethical consent✓ Met
14 interviews conducted and documented (12 in-person, 2 via video call). All participants received the consent information sheet before interview; verbal consent was recorded at the start of each session. Three contributors requested anonymisation (family names not used in the cookbook); this was honoured in all cases. Interview summaries and anonymised transcript excerpts are in Appendix B; consent documentation is in Appendix C.
Criterion 4: Original food photography for every recipe✓ Met
14 dish photographs taken by the student. 11 photographs were taken in contributors' homes during or immediately after cooking; 3 were taken in the school food technology kitchen after the contributor helped the student prepare the dish on site. All photographs are original. I note that the quality of the 3 school-kitchen photographs is slightly lower than the home photographs — home cooking environments provided better natural light — but all 14 are usable and clearly original.
Criterion 5: Production cost ≤ AED 350 for 6 copies✓ Met
Total production cost: AED 312 for 6 copies (AED 52 per copy). This included professional printing (120gsm interior, laminated card cover) and perfect binding from a Dubai print shop. Itemised breakdown in Appendix D.
Criterion 6: ≥4 families confirm accurate representation✓ Met
8 of the 14 contributing families responded to the accuracy check email. All 8 confirmed the dish was accurately represented. One family (Lebanon contributor) requested a small correction to the dialect transliteration of the dish name — this was corrected before the final print run. The 6 families who did not respond may reflect email inbox issues or busy schedules rather than dissatisfaction; none contacted me to raise a concern. I consider 8 responses an adequate sample, though a higher response rate would have provided stronger evidence.
✓ What Makes This Evaluation Strong
  • Criterion 2 is honestly reported as "mostly met" — not inflated to "fully met"
  • Specific explanation for why two sections are academically weaker (limited scholarly literature)
  • Criterion 4 note about photograph quality variation — shows critical eye
  • Ci Impact 2 connects personal discovery (own family history) to the intellectual framework of the project — not just "this was nice"
✗ What to Avoid
  • "The cookbook turned out beautiful and everyone loved it" — aesthetic praise, no criterion evaluation
  • Claiming all criteria fully met without evidence
  • Ci that lists positive experiences only — IB moderators consistently note that reflections showing discomfort, challenge, or revision are more credible than uniformly positive ones
